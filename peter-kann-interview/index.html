Peter R. Kann: A Life on the Front Lines From the halls of Harvard to the jungles of Indochina — a Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist looks back on reporting across Asia's most turbulent decades. Interviewer: Anindo Atik • Interviewee: Peter Kann, The Wall Street Journal INTRODUCTION The Library of Congress in Washington, D.C., has a way of changing lives — sometimes simply by who you sit next to in the research room. It was sometime between 2013 and 2015. I had become a fixture in the research section, always first through the doors, last to leave. I was deep into my documentary on the Bangladesh Liberation War when an elderly man at the next table struck up a conversation. Friendly, unassuming. Just another researcher, or so I thought. “What are you working on?” he asked. When I told him I was researching a documentary film about the Bangladesh Liberation War, his eyes lit up. He turned out to be a retired Wall Street Journal journalist. “Do you know about ‘Dacca Diary’?” I didn’t. “You need to look into it,” he said. “And Peter Kann — the journalist who wrote it — won the Pulitzer Prize for covering your war in 1971.” Later, I learned that Peter R. Kann is the only journalist to win a major award for his writing on the 1971 War. This is what happens in Washington: You never know who is sitting at the next table. That chance encounter set me on a trail to find Peter R. Kann — a name that should be known in Bangladesh but had somehow slipped into obscurity. Here was an American journalist who witnessed the birth of a nation, who refused evacuation orders to stay and document history, and who won journalism’s highest honor for his coverage of the 1971 war. Yet for decades, his work had been largely forgotten in the very country whose story he told so powerfully. In 1972, Peter R. Kann received the Pulitzer Prize for International Reporting, recognizing his extraordinary dispatches from the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War. But his story represents something larger — a vanishing breed of journalism in which correspondents could spend months immersed in a single story, prioritizing depth over daily deadlines and truth over speed. ￼ Peter R. Kann — Pulitzer Prize-winning foreign correspondent, The Wall Street Journal. Photo: Wikispooks, CC BY-SA. Born in 1942 in Princeton, New Jersey, to Austrian Jewish immigrants who had fled the Nazis, Kann developed an inexplicable obsession with newspapers from childhood. At ten, he was already publishing his own neighborhood newspaper, going door-to-door as reporter, editor, and salesman. His father, a distinguished historian, hoped he might pursue law or diplomacy. But Kann’s attention span favored “the daily churn of news” over the patient scholarship of academia. After Harvard — where he spent more time at The Harvard Crimson than in classrooms — Kann’s path to journalism took unexpected turns. Four months driving a bulldozer at the Cambridge city dump. Covering the steel industry in Pittsburgh. Reporting on drug trafficking in Mexico. Each assignment was preparation for what would become his true calling: becoming a foreign correspondent in Asia. By 1967, at age twenty-three, Kann arrived in Saigon as The Wall Street Journal’s first resident reporter in Vietnam. What followed was a twelve-year odyssey across Southeast Asia — through the remote valleys of Borneo and New Guinea and into the heart of the Indian subcontinent. When East Pakistan erupted in March 1971, Kann initially knew little about the region. But his commitment to the story became legendary. He spent months traveling between refugee camps in West Bengal, government offices in Delhi and Islamabad, and eventually the embattled territory of Dhaka. Then, in December 1971, Pakistani forces collapsed and Indian troops closed in on Dhaka. His editors in New York sent urgent cables: evacuate immediately. Kann sent back a message claiming their order had been “unreceived.” At twenty-nine, despite every warning, he witnessed the birth of Bangladesh on Liberation Day. His “Dacca Diary” would become one of the era’s defining pieces of war correspondence — and earn him the Pulitzer Prize. That journalistic career would eventually lead him to become the CEO and Chairman of Dow Jones & Company, the publisher of The Wall Street Journal. But it all began with a young reporter who understood that some stories are worth the risk and that witnessing history matters more than following orders. What follows is a conversation with that reporter, conducted on June 6, 2017. ABOUT PETER R. KANN Peter R. Kann (born 1942) is an American journalist, editor, and businessman. In 1967, he became The Wall Street Journal’s first resident reporter in Vietnam, and subsequently served as a roving correspondent across Asia, based in Hong Kong. In 1972, he was awarded the Pulitzer Prize for distinguished international reporting for his coverage of the Bangladesh Liberation War. He later became publisher of The Wall Street Journal (1988) and chairman and CEO of Dow Jones & Company (1992–2006). He serves as adjunct faculty at the Columbia University Graduate School of Journalism. SECTION I Early Life and Discovering Journalism ANINDO ATIK Can you start by talking about your childhood? PETER KANN I grew up in Princeton, New Jersey, a very pretty college town. My parents were immigrants from Vienna, Austria, who had left Austria when the Nazis came in. So, I ended up having a largely fortunate and very happy childhood in this town. My passion as a teenager, but even before that, was newspapers. That's been sort of a lifelong passion of mine. My primary passion, I think, as a young person growing up was newspapering, was journalism. When I was ten years old, I actually published my own neighborhood newspaper. So, I was the reporter and the editor and the salesman. I would go door to door and ask the neighbors questions: "What happened in your family this week?" I would record it, and my poor mother would have to type the newspaper on an old manual typewriter with carbon paper. And then I would distribute those carbons around the neighborhood for, I think, five cents a copy. When I was a little older than that, as a teenager, I began working for the town newspaper, the Princeton Packet, as well as for my school newspaper. I suppose whereas most of my fellow teenagers had heroes who were baseball players or football players, my heroes tended to be journalists and writers. James Reston of The New York Times, for example, or among authors I loved, and I still do, George Orwell, partly because he writes so simply and elegantly. He never uses a long word when a short word will do just as well or better, and partly because of all the adventures he had in his life, like covering the Spanish Civil War in the '30s. I've asked myself since, a little boy, why was I so attracted to newspapers? I think in part this was a reaction to the life my father led. My father was a historian, a university professor, and he spent weeks and months and years working on a manuscript about Austrian history, for example. That took an enormous amount of discipline and dedication, and I had neither of those. My attention span tended to be in hours or days, not months and years. And of course, journalism is natural for that. Every day is new news, and every day is a new newspaper. I think my father was not particularly pleased with my evolution into a professional journalist. He always thought I would have been better off being a lawyer or a diplomat or something respectable, which journalism wasn't quite. But in any case, he happily let me pursue my own interests. He was a professor at Rutgers most of his life, also worked for some years at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. And then later, when he retired at Rutgers, he became a professor at the University of Vienna. So, he actually returned to the country that he had emigrated from. “When I was ten years old, I actually published my own neighborhood newspaper. So, I was the reporter and the editor and the salesman.” — PETER KANN SECTION II Family Background and Jewish Identity ANINDO ATIK Since your parents emigrated from Austria and were Jewish, what was it like in your family after the Second World War? While growing up, did you experience discrimination in the USA? PETER KANN My parents had emigrated from Austria in 1938, first to England and then on to America. The main reason they left Austria, or had to leave, was that much of the family was Jewish. So, it would have been suicidal to stay. They were fortunate to be able to get out. I was raised here (in America). I came of age, that I can remember, in the mid to late 1940s. I do remember that my parents, who spoke German to each other at home and often spoke German to me, did not really want me speaking German back. They did not want a little five-year-old boy going to nursery school speaking German. If there was any discrimination, it would have been against German. I recall no discrimination at all against Jews or against immigrants. And actually, I was raised in a Methodist Church, so that was sort of a non-issue in my childhood. ANINDO ATIK Did your parents speak English? PETER KANN They spoke German, French, and English. SECTION III Harvard and Early Career ANINDO ATIK Walk us through your college education and job. Where did you initially work? PETER KANN Through college, I spent much more time — I went to Harvard, and I probably spent three or four times a week, as much time on the college newspaper there, the Crimson, than I spent on my studies. But that was fun, and I had adventures there. I got to interview President Kennedy just a few weeks before he was assassinated. I covered local politics in Cambridge and state politics and to some extent even national politics. So, Harvard was great fun, but primarily because of the journalism. When I graduated from Harvard in 1964, all I knew was I wanted to get a really good newspaper job and ideally to get one in Asia, which seemed to me remote and exotic and exciting. So, I wrote letters to all kinds of English language newspapers in Hong Kong and Singapore and predictably got no response. So, I went to work in the Cambridge, Massachusetts city dumps for four months, driving a bulldozer to put out fires in the dump. I thought, well, maybe I can write a book about this. But of course, I never did. And I think after four months I realized that working in a dump was not a very good return on my parents' investment in my college education. So, I then actually took a job with the Wall Street Journal. SECTION IV The Path to Asia ANINDO ATIK You spent about twelve years in Southeast Asia, from 1967 to 1979. Why Southeast Asia? After getting an education at Harvard, most people think about the corporate world, higher salary. What drew you to Southeast Asia? PETER KANN I absolutely knew I wanted to be a journalist. I think I had known that from age 9 or 10. The appeal of Asia, which came to me primarily in my late years at Harvard, was just the idea of someplace remote and exciting. I'd read about Asia but had never been there. So, I had this vision in my mind that that's where I would like to be a foreign correspondent and have adventures. It took me a few years to get there, but I did manage to. I went to work for the Wall Street Journal after my months in the city dump. The Wall Street Journal first sent me to Pittsburgh, which was not a glamorous place or glamorous job — writing about the steel industry and coal miners and so on. But after about nine months, they moved me to Los Angeles. So, I thought, well, at least that's getting a little closer to Asia. I spent about 18 months in Los Angeles. During that period, I covered the gambling industry in Las Vegas. I covered the film industry in California. I took a long trip to Alaska. I took a trip, or more than one, to Mexico writing about the drug industry and how the farmers in Sinaloa and the mountains would grow the opium poppies, for which they got a few pennies for a kilo. And then how one could trace the progress of that substance through to the labs in Mazatlán on the Mexican coast and then across the border. What the peasant would have gotten pennies for was worth a million dollars on the street corners of Los Angeles. I think perhaps because of that story, which was somewhat dramatic and a little bit dangerous, the Wall Street Journal finally said, "You want to go to Asia? We're willing to send you. You can go to Vietnam." And for me, that was perfect. Vietnam by 1967 was a major war. There were almost half a million American troops. It was in the news every day. And that's really where I wanted to be, where the action was. So, in a way, one day I was leading a rather peaceful life in Los Angeles, and a week later, I was in Saigon. ANINDO ATIK Were you afraid to go to Vietnam? PETER KANN No. My parents were not happy about it, but I was very happy about it. I think back, why did they send me? One of the reasons maybe was that story I wrote in Mexico. Probably also, I was young — I was 23 years old. I was single, I was unattached other than to my parents, and I was inexpensive. I mean, I was probably earning $200 a week or something like that. I wouldn't say I was expendable, but it wasn't a big risk for the Wall Street Journal to send me there. And I didn't see it as a big personal risk either. I just thought of it as, wow, I'm now about to start the adventurous part of my life. And I think that's what happened. “One day I was leading a rather peaceful life in Los Angeles, and a week later, I was in Saigon.” — PETER KANN ￼ Nguyen Hue Boulevard, Saigon, mid-1960s — The city Kann described as one side of a “schizophrenic life.”  Photo: Gene Long / manhhai via Saigoneer. SECTION V Vietnam — The Schizophrenic Life ANINDO ATIK When you first arrived in Saigon, what was your impression? PETER KANN My first impression of Vietnam was, and I think this is probably true of most conflict situations like that one, I was about to lead a somewhat schizophrenic life. Saigon in the late '60s was not exactly a pleasant place. It was full of refugees and there was poverty and very occasionally a little bit of danger. But the war, except for Tet in 1968, had never really touched Saigon itself. So, one could lead a quite normal life in Saigon. There were pretty good French restaurants. There were lots of other journalists. You could play poker in the evenings. There were bars. There were pretty girls. So that was one side of the schizophrenic life. The other side was you could leave Saigon, either going out with American troop units, and then you really took as much risk as you chose to take. If you went out to a division headquarters, you took no real risk at all. If you went to a brigade, hardly any risk. Down to a battalion, yes, they might get mortared or shot at. And then if you went out with a rifle platoon, then you were taking real risk. Or you could travel around the country, which I did more of, not embedded in a troop unit, but really on your own. I particularly liked the Mekong Delta south of Saigon because there were the fewest American troops there. It was still more of a Vietnamese war, and one could write about not just the war, but about the Vietnamese and how the war was affecting them. So, I would travel through the Mekong Delta for weeks at a time, sometimes on buses and boats and occasionally a car and stay in villages. That's a lot of what I wrote about in Vietnam — the Vietnamese and the Vietnamese war. I also did a little bit of the other kind of reporting. I went to Khe Sanh. I saw body bags and all the rest of it. But I got very engaged in Vietnam and the Vietnamese, and that's mostly what I wrote about there. And then after a week in the field or a week roaming the Mekong Delta, you could come back to Saigon and stay in a perfectly nice old French hotel and go out to dinner with some friends and have a good French dinner. And that was the schizophrenia of it. SECTION VI Cambodia and Laos ANINDO ATIK Were you ever in Cambodia? PETER KANN The war in Cambodia, which began in the early '70s, was a very different kind of war. I was also there and in Laos. But Cambodia, there were no American troops whatsoever. Cambodia for many years had been a little peaceful kind of fairytale kingdom ruled by Prince Sihanouk. In part, it remained peaceful because Sihanouk turned a blind eye to the North Vietnamese sanctuaries in eastern Cambodia. At some point, the South Vietnamese and the Americans probably encouraged a coup in Cambodia. Sihanouk was overthrown by Lon Nol, a general, and then Cambodia went to war. But as I say, it was a totally different kind of war from Vietnam. There were no major battles. There were no foreign troops. It was Cambodians against Cambodians — the Khmer Rouge against the Cambodian government. To cover that war, one would stay in Phnom Penh. The Hotel Royale was the place most journalists stayed. You would get in a car in the morning and travel out on any one of the four or five roads leading out of Phnom Penh like spokes from a hub. You'd get in a car with a driver, travel down one of those roads until you found the war. And you knew you had found the war when suddenly the farmers disappeared from the fields. So, you'd look around and there'd be no water buffaloes, no farmers — just kind of deathly silence. That meant you'd actually come to the front line on that road. And if you drove another kilometer or two further on, you wound up in the territory of the Khmer Rouge, and you wound up dead. There were substantially more journalists killed in Cambodia than in Vietnam, mostly just by that careless mistake of not noticing that the water buffaloes weren't in the fields and driving a kilometer too far. So actually, Cambodia was somewhat frightening to cover. Sadly, for the Cambodians, the war ended with the Khmer Rouge winning. It was bad enough in Vietnam when the communists won, with reeducation camps and boat people. But in Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge conservatively killed a third of the population of the country, mostly brutally, either by starvation or by beating people to death. So that was both a scary and enormously sad war to cover. Laos was the third piece of old French Indochina. Journalists tended to see it as kind of a comic opera war. And to some extent it was. I remember writing a story about the Royal Laotian Navy, which had all kinds of admirals and captains, but it had no boats and it had no ocean, only the Mekong River drifting by. There were all kinds of odd things in Laos. Opium was legal in Laos. So, journalists at the end of the day could go and smoke an opium pipe. Because it was legal, I can admit I did, but not very often. But it wound up in the end as a very sad story. Journalists have a tendency to typecast countries. Cambodia initially was fairytale kingdom. Laos was kind of comic opera. And of course, for the people of Cambodia and the people of Laos, there was nothing comic about it at all. It was serious and a lot of them died. ANINDO ATIK Did you know Sydney Schanberg during that time? PETER KANN He was somewhat older. He was based in India for a while. I knew him in Saigon and a little bit in Cambodia. “You knew you had found the war when suddenly the farmers disappeared from the fields. You’d look around and there’d be no water buffaloes, no farmers — just kind of deathly silence.” — PETER KANN, ON COVERING CAMBODIA SECTION VII Hong Kong and Roving Asia ANINDO ATIK Then you went to Hong Kong? PETER KANN From 1967 to the end of '69, I was resident in Saigon and spent all my time in Indochina. In 1970, I was transferred to Hong Kong as sort of the roving Asia correspondent for the Wall Street Journal. So, I suppose it was a promotion. I went from covering three countries to covering 15 countries, but I still was responsible for Vietnam and Cambodia and Laos. So, I'd split my time. I would probably spend a month in Vietnam or Cambodia, then a few weeks in Hong Kong, and then I would fly off for some adventure somewhere else in Asia. So, I went to Irian Jaya on the Indonesian half of New Guinea and wrote about tribesmen in mountain valleys. I went up the rivers in Borneo and wrote about primitive life there. I started going to the subcontinent then. Probably in 1969 or '70, I got very intrigued with Calcutta. ANINDO ATIK You visited Calcutta many times? PETER KANN I think I went there in '69 for the first time. And I sort of fell in love with Calcutta. Many people found it horrifying because of the overt poverty. And it is true — you could see bodies on the streets in the morning. But at the same time, it was the most intellectually exciting city in Asia. The Bengalis in particular are so voluble and talkative. Almost anyone you met, you wound up in a lively conversation with, that ranged from the Naxalites in the coffee shops of Presidency College to the politicians around Jyoti Basu. I remember having dinner with the Maharani of Cooch Behar in a sort of tumbledown palace in Calcutta. SECTION VIII Mother Teresa in Calcutta ￼ Mother Teresa, photographed during a visit to Bonn, 1986. Photo: Türelio, CC 2.0 via Nobel Prize Outreach. PETER KANN I met Mother Teresa. I met all kinds of interesting people there. I could tell you one, sort of amusing story, about Mother Teresa. ANINDO ATIK Please do. PETER KANN So, I went with another journalist and asked for an interview with her, and the answer from her assistant was, "She will see you, but first you have to go and visit her works" — by which she meant the leper colony, the home for the dying, the orphanage, and so on. So, we spent some days going around and visiting her works. In a way, the most impressive was the home for the dying, because the nuns would pick these near corpses up from the street in the morning and bring them in and bathe them and tend them and feed them soup. It was probably the first time in their lives that anyone had shown them any kind of kindness. And then within days, most of them died. So, it really was a home for the dying. Anyway, so then we went back to Mother Teresa, and she granted us the interview. She asked, "Did you visit our works?" And I said, "Yes, we did," and I described how impressed we were. And then she said, "So what are you going to do?" This was kind of an open-ended question. What do you say? So, we figured, well, maybe what she wanted was a contribution. So, we reached in our pockets and brought out traveler's checks — that's what you used in those days. And we signed the traveler's checks and put them in a stack on her desk, and she pushed them aside and said, "What are you going to do?" Of course, she was perfectly happy to take the contribution. But what she meant was, what are we going to do with our lives that will help the people that she helps. And I've always remembered that. Plus, she had these piercing blue eyes, so that made it memorable. “What are you going to do?” — MOTHER TERESA, TO PETER KANN AFTER HE OFFERED A MONETARY DONATION. WHAT SHE MEANT WAS: WHAT WOULD HE DO WITH HIS LIFE. SECTION IX Pakistan — The Northwest Frontier ANINDO ATIK You also traveled extensively in Pakistan? PETER KANN Yes, I also got fascinated by Pakistan, by the more remote regions of Pakistan. In the early '70s — some of these trips were just before the Bangladesh war, some after — I spent some weeks in Baluchistan. I spent some weeks in Waziristan, the northwest frontier, at least a week or two in Hunza and Gilgit, in the Himalayas, in the Hindu Kush. ANINDO ATIK Why the northwest frontier? PETER KANN I'd read Kipling. So, if you read Kipling, you found the northwest frontier romantic. And then there was a British writer named George MacDonald Fraser who wrote a whole series of sort of comic novels called Flashman. A lot of them were set in the Northwest Frontier. So that was kind of romantic. ANINDO ATIK That is fascinating. Tell me more about West Pakistan. PETER KANN You'd fly to Karachi and then on to Islamabad, and most of these were closed areas. Waziristan, North and South Waziristan was very much a closed area. I got permission to go from Bhutto, who was then the prime minister. That would have been just after the Bangladesh war. The same thing happened with Hunza, which was also closed. I'd say something like, "Well, I really am so fascinated by your country. Are there parts that are restrictive?" And of course, if you say that to a president, it's a little bit embarrassing. So, he has to say, "Well, nothing is really restricted. Where do you want to go?" And then I would say, "Well, I'd like to go to North and South Waziristan." And I was the first foreigner who traveled through that area in at least ten years. You had old British bases on the frontier, separated by 20 or 30 kilometers. Everything else was owned by the tribes — the Mahsuds. The Pakistan military hardly ever left their little compounds. But within the compounds, this was funny, they essentially led the same life that the British had led in those same little bases 40 or 50 years before. There was one called Miram Shah, I think. I arrived by jeep in Miram Shah and I was met by the colonel who was in charge of the little base on the frontier — that area that's now Taliban territory. So, I was met by the colonel and he offered me a cold shower. And then he said, "Well, you must join us for dinner in the mess." And I said, "Oh, very nice of you, sir. I'd be happy to." And he said, "Will you be dressing for dinner?" Now, what do you say to that? I had a pair of khaki pants, and I think I had one clean shirt, or almost clean shirt. So, I said, "Well, yes, sir." And I show up at the Officers' Mess in my shirt and trousers, and all the officers are in full dress uniform. And the colonel is obviously devastated. He expected me to show up in a dinner jacket. So, he said, "I thought you said you would be able to dress for dinner." And I said, "Well, sorry, I misunderstood. This is all I have." And they said, "Well, never mind." So, the whole group of officers leave the mess, and they go to put on their camouflage uniforms and come back. And we had a very nice dinner. I think I asked the mess steward — he was an old man, well up in his 80s, who had been serving drinks in the mess very much since the British days — I asked him, "So what did the British do here in the evenings in this lonely place?" And he said, "Buggery, sir. Much buggery." ANINDO ATIK You also traveled to Hunza several times? PETER KANN Probably a week or ten days in Hunza. I went several times, actually. I took my wife there for a honeymoon. We traveled through Swat to Gilgit and then to Hunza, and then by jeep from Hunza through Nagar and Yasin and all these little principalities to Chitral. And then down from there, it was like a 20-hour jeep ride back to Rawalpindi and Islamabad. So, I lived this rather strange life where I was still at this time covering Vietnam part-time. I was living in Hong Kong, even though it was more a place to change clothes and rest a little bit. And then I was going off and having adventures, I guess you'd call it, around Asia. SECTION X East Pakistan — March to July 1971 ANINDO ATIK You have been to many Southeast Asian countries. Had you ever visited East Pakistan before 1971? PETER KANN The one country in Asia I had not been to, was Bangladesh, was East Pakistan. I knew West Pakistan pretty well. I knew India pretty well. I'd been to Burma, Nepal. But I had never been to East Pakistan at this time. I think I probably had somewhat the stereotype that the West Pakistanis had. The West Pakistanis viewed themselves as the strong martial people in the British tradition, and the military was very central to the whole society. And their view of Bengal, of East Pakistan, was, "Well, there are a lot of little brown people there. They talk a lot and they don't really do anything. And every once in a while, there are floods and then they get killed and then they repopulate again." I mean, it was a very dismissive, kind of racist view of what was actually more than half their own country, because East Pakistan had a larger population than the West. I'm not saying I believed all that, but there wasn't anything that particularly drew me to East Pakistan. If I wanted to write about Bengali culture, I could do that from Calcutta. There was no reason to go to Dhaka. So, then that would bring us to the spring of 1971, when I knew there had been all kinds of political turmoil in East Pakistan surrounding the election there. Then when I heard in late March of '71 that East Pakistan had declared independence, or maybe it was mid-March, I went off to Calcutta. But at that point you could not enter — a journalist couldn't enter East Pakistan. ANINDO ATIK So, you were not in East Pakistan in March? PETER KANN No, no. In March, I was in West Bengal. And that's where I learned about the army crackdown in East Pakistan. And then within days, the refugees started coming across the border. So, I spent a fair amount of time along the Bengal border talking to refugees. They all had these horror stories about what the army was doing. And then some weeks later, you started being able to interview some of the young people who were the beginnings of the Mukti Bahini. But East Pakistan at that point was a closed area to journalists. You could follow what was going on by going to some of the embassies or consulates in West Bengal, and they could tell you what was being reported to them from their colleagues in Dhaka, but no firsthand knowledge. I think I finally got in there in July, perhaps. Some other journalists and I got into East Pakistan at the point at which the Pakistan Army believed they had put down the rebellion. So, then they were sort of proud to show off, "This is all very peaceful here." I was also in the border areas, and you could walk across the border for three or four miles. And what you basically saw were more refugees on their way out. During that same couple of months — so May, June — I went to Delhi to try to understand how the Indians viewed what was going on in East Pakistan. And I went to Islamabad and wrote about the dilemma that the Pakistan government had. What were they going to do? They weren't going to let East Pakistan go. They were trying to put down a rebellion. Usually that doesn't work very well. I also wrote a little bit about the great power politics of it. The Soviets supporting India, India supporting the Bengalis, and the U.S. and China supporting West Pakistan, because all that was happening at that time. By the time I was able to get back into East Pakistan, it was probably late July. And at that point, the cities were certainly under Pakistani military control. But that was delusional. As soon as you got in the car and drove out from Dhaka, you saw Pakistan Army people on the roads, and beyond that, they had no real presence at all. They would occasionally launch raids in the villages and kill people and burn them. But the countryside was essentially trying to evade the Pakistan Army. And the army's control was towns and roads, and that was it. SECTION XI Inside East Pakistan — Fear and Violence PETER KANN What I mostly remember from those months, from summer to basically the end of the year — I wasn't there all the time, but I was in and out — what I remembered was just the amount of fear in Bengali society, more so than I'd ever seen anywhere else. If you drove out of Dhaka and stopped in a town and tried to interview people, most people didn't dare speak to you. They would avert their eyes. If you finally got into someone's house and they were willing to talk, they would talk in a whisper, and their hands would be shaking. And that was real. I remember some Bengali doctor telling me, in effect, he was risking his life to talk to me at all. And some Hindu girl who told the story of how a Pakistani officer had made her hold the barrel of his gun and said, "It's still warm, isn't it?" She said yes. And he said, "From killing Hindus." I mean, there was just a lot of terrible stuff went on. Not all of it was done by the Pakistan Army. There were all these vigilante groups like the Razakars, who may have been more brutal than the military. There were the Biharis killing Bengalis. There were, to some extent, Bengalis trying to take retribution on Biharis. There were feuds within villages. So, there was just sort of omnipresent violence for that nine months in a society that was already poor. “If you drove out of Dhaka and stopped in a town and tried to interview people, most people didn’t dare speak to you. They would avert their eyes. If you finally got into someone’s house… they would talk in a whisper, and their hands would be shaking.” — PETER KANN, ON REPORTING INSIDE EAST PAKISTAN, 1971 ANINDO ATIK How far did you travel in Bangladesh? PETER KANN I went first north to Tangail, sort of a windy route, but I think we ended up in Tangail or actually in a rainforest near there where there was a little Catholic, Roman Catholic mission. On that trip, we would stop and try to find someone who would talk to you and tell you what had been going on. Sometimes people would, but they were brave people to do it. As foreigners, you stood out — you couldn't anonymously talk to anyone. We were followed by these Razakars, vigilantes. And they were really nasty people. I don't know what they would have done to us, but they certainly were following us, and I assume they thought we were spies. So, then we found this little Catholic mission. There was a missionary called Father Hemrick, who ministered to a tribe called the Garo, who lived in the jungle. And he actually hid us. He camouflaged our car. He hid us in this little mission house. And it probably overstates it to say he saved our lives, but he certainly saved us from a lot of unpleasantness. So, I've always admired him, and ever since I've contributed to his mission. I thought it was the least I could do. So that was one expedition sort of north of Dhaka. Then I did a trip by boat to what was it, Munshiganj or something? That was like 30, 40 miles to the south. That was later. That was when actually the Mukti Bahini controlled that area. That was in early December, maybe, or late November. And that was fascinating because, I was with a couple of other journalists, we arrived and we were greeted like visiting VIPs, and there were Bangladesh flags flying and there were speeches. And this was still in a country where it wasn't yet a free country. I mean, the Army in theory could have walked in at any time. But I think the army by then was so stretched out and so confined to their cantonments that what went on in the countryside was almost irrelevant to them. I did another road trip going west from Dhaka for a couple of days to the Jamuna River. This was probably also in December, early December. There were rumors that the Indian Army was entering East Pakistan. I think it hadn't actually entered yet. But you kept hearing these rumors in Dhaka. So, a couple of journalists and I decided we'd go see. So, we got to the banks of the Jamuna. And there was one ferryboat that was still operating. The river must have been, I don't know, two miles across. Bangladesh, as you know, has these enormous rivers, more like oceans. The ferryboat — there had been three ferry boats and two of them had been sunk by Indian planes. So, this was either just when the war was starting or just before. And my friends wanted to go across the river and find the war. And I said, "I'm not going to get on this last ferry boat. Either it's going to be sunk in the middle of the river, or we're going to get across and then it'll be sunk and we'll never get back." So, they were kind of crazy and they did it. They crossed on the ferry boat. I was cowardly, but as it turned out, smart, because I turned around and headed back to Dhaka — first by motorized scooter, then by pedal rickshaw, and then walking for a while. But I finally at night got back to Dhaka. My friends who had crossed the river wound up getting back a week later, by which time they'd missed quite a bit of the actual war. So those are just the kind of decisions you make as a journalist. Some wise, some foolish. ￼ Indian troops on the eastern front in Bangladesh, December 1971. | Wikimedia Commons / LiveHistoryIndia SECTION XII December 1971 — The War and Liberation PETER KANN In early December of '71 is when you could see the end of the story approaching. So, I was obviously there then. I may have been there for much of November too. You kind of knew the current situation couldn't go on. There was too much political pressure on Mrs. Gandhi to do something, with 12 million refugees on the border. And the Pakistan Army, I think I wrote about this — I had this feeling they knew at a certain point they were going to lose East Pakistan. But I think they realized: how would you rather lose? Would you rather lose to the Mukti Bahini, which I think was the worst option for them. These little brown people who they disparaged couldn't beat them in their view. Or would you rather lose in a conventional war with the Indian Army, where at least you can say the Indian Army has three times as many troops and more planes and more guns and more artillery? And that's what they did. I mean, I don't know who fired the first shot, but in a way, both India and Pakistan had logical reason to go to war to get this thing over with. And they did. And then, of course, once that war started, the journalists — however many there were of us, 15 or 20 — were in the Intercontinental Hotel. And that was a rather strange existence too, because as that month went on, you couldn't file stories. The patent office was mostly closed. There was censorship. So, what I mostly did was write a diary, which I then managed to get published partly during the war and partly after. The Intercontinental had maybe 20 journalists. It had various diplomat families who were there. It started then to get Pakistani rich Pakistanis who thought this was the safest place to be if things went badly for them, which, predictably, they would. But they often came in with guns. So, the journalists became the enforcers of the so-called peace zone, or whatever the hotel was called. So, we'd have to go room to room and confiscate weapons from Pakistanis. And then at a certain point, we had to guard the gate so that people, non-foreigners essentially, couldn't come in. Again, one of those situations which are not exactly just, where the small group of foreigners could lead a relatively privileged life, staying in a sanctuary hotel. And everyone else in this society was subject to being killed or killing each other. But that's the way it was. ANINDO ATIK I know from your writing that you spent a night on a boat trip, and they gave you food to eat, and you made some comment, and then they would complain that the honored guest had been given better food — a piece of chicken or something. PETER KANN Right, right. You know, with any guerrilla army, you had some serious guerrillas — often those were people who had at least been in the Pakistan Army or the Pakistan Police force and, of course, had been discriminated against by the West Pakistanis and joined the revolution. But then there were all these enthusiastic university kids who joined the revolution, some of them serious, some of them probably less so. And then you always have kind of flaky hangers-on. Yeah, I remember there was one kid who was showing off his gun and telling me how he had "operated on ten Pakistanis," by which he meant, "I have killed ten Pakistanis." And I said, "How many prisoners did you take?" And he said, "No prisoners. We kill them all." So, he was very fierce. And then I said, "Well, I hope your new country turns out all right and lives in peace with its neighbors." And he said, "Oh, no, our new country is going to include West Bengal." And I said, "Really? I'm not sure the Indians will be so excited to hear that." And he said, "And Assam too." And I said, "Oh, what about Tripura?" And he said, "Yes, sure. Tripura too." So, he had this great vision of, you know, Azad Bangladesh or something. And so there was this humor in the midst of war. And that was funny. But there were many stories that were not funny. ￼ Lt. Gen. A.A.K. Niazi and Lt. Gen. Jagjit Singh Aurora sign the Pakistani Instrument of Surrender in Dhaka — 16 December 1971, the day Kann witnessed as Liberation Day. | Wikimedia Commons / LiveHistoryIndia SECTION XIII December 16, 1971 — Liberation Day ANINDO ATIK Were you still in Bangladesh after the war ended? Where were you during that time? PETER KANN When the war ended — so, whatever that was, December 16th — I had two main memories. One was of a father and his son, a Bengali father and son. The boy was probably four years old, and they were both out in the streets yelling "Jai Bangla! Jai Bangla!" as you would expect, along with most everyone else. And I said to the father, "So your little boy is very happy." And he said, "This is the first time in nine months that my son has been outside of the house. Because he learned the phrase 'Jai Bangla' back when the revolution began, when the independence movement began. And after March 25th, if you said that in the streets you could be killed or your family could be killed. So, they had locked the kid up so he couldn't possibly doom the family by saying 'Jai Bangla.'" And the other memory that has always stuck with me was when the Indian army came into Dhaka, there were crowds, mobs in the streets, mostly friendly, celebrating. Some were looking for Razakars and enemies, but mostly very happy. The Indian officers would be moving through the streets, and the crowds would descend on them, mostly to thank them and celebrate them. But there were also defeated Pakistani troops wandering around who didn't know where to go, who to surrender to. And some of those were officers too. So, I remember these Indian officers with their swagger sticks, beating the Bengalis whom they had just liberated in order to keep the Bengalis from beating up the defeated Pakistanis. And of course, that also takes you back to the British era. I mean, these — at least the more senior Indian and Pakistani officers — had all gone to the same schools, had gone to Dehradun or, in some cases, Sandhurst. And they had much more in common with each other than had with the liberated people of Bangladesh. I think I even quoted some of them saying that. So, there were a lot of ironies. But at least the war ended. And there was some bloodshed after the war, but I thought remarkably little considering how brutal the Pakistanis had been. “This is the first time in nine months that my son has been outside of the house.” — A BENGALI FATHER ON THE STREETS OF DHAKA, DECEMBER 16, 1971 — LIBERATION DAY SECTION XIV On Genocide and Casualties ANINDO ATIK When you say "genocide," what do you mean by that? You've referred to it in your articles. PETER KANN I would say there was a kind of genocide taking place, but it was not uniformly applied, if that's the right word. The comparison with the Nazis and the Jews isn't actually an accurate comparison. I mean, the Nazis were simply trying to wipe out an entire race of people. And there have been other examples like that. On a smaller scale, the Nigerians trying to totally wipe out the Igbo tribe. To me, that's kind of classic genocide, where a racial or ethnic group is identified for extermination. That wasn't exactly what was happening in East Pakistan. The Pakistan Army, as brutal and stupid as it was, was not trying to exterminate the Bengali people. Secondly, there was a lot of other killing going on. There was killing of the Bihari minority and Bengalis. There was killing going on between Muslims and Hindus, including sometimes among Bengalis themselves, because the Hindus were a tempting target for everyone. And there was random violence going on. So, I've always kind of shied a little bit away from the genocide phrase. But I think there's absolutely no doubt that there was a horrendous amount of killing going on, and that was primarily being done by the Pakistan military. Some of it very conscious deciding "we're going to destroy a village; we're going to kill all the Hindus." Some of it done out of kind of ignorance. I mean, if you talk to some of the Pakistan Army recruits — these were farm kids from somewhere in the Punjab. They had no idea why they were there. They had no idea basically who the Bengalis were, even though they were, in theory, citizens of the same country. They tended to be told that they were there fighting Indians, i.e. Hindus, and "miscreants." So, a certain amount of this was just being done by dumb, brutal kids put in a situation where they were scared. So, I'll never defend what the Pakistan Army did. But was it genocide or was it simply terrible slaughtering of people? Somewhere in between. ANINDO ATIK You wrote about an American woman who said a Pakistani officer told her, "They are committing genocide," and when she asked if he was upset about it, he said they should be doing more of it. PETER KANN Yeah. That was a chilling quote. She was probably in the hotel because I didn't have any contact with Americans outside the hotel. So, she must have been one of the diplomats' wives or an NGO wife or something. From the Pakistani — "We should have done more of that" or "We should do more of it." People like that exist. They existed high up in the Pakistan chain of command. ANINDO ATIK What about the numbers? The dispute over casualties? PETER KANN The numbers will probably never be known. The same thing during World War II with the Holocaust — more people were affected, but exact numbers remain debated. We were talking about Cambodia before — no one will ever quite know how many people the Khmer Rouge killed. The common estimates are a third of the population, maybe 2 million. In Bangladesh, I've heard numbers like 500,000, 600,000. That's probably the low end. At the very high end, 3 million. But I don't think there'll ever be an accurate count. And that in itself is frightening. SECTION XV On Memory, Trauma, and Nations Forgetting ANINDO ATIK Sydney Schanberg told me that Americans don't know what war is like because we haven't experienced war on our soil since the Civil War. He said once it happens here, only then will we understand and prevent our government from supporting wars abroad. What's your take? PETER KANN That's kind of a profound historical question. Look, I think there are societies that go through very traumatic events. Bangladesh is one of them. Cambodia is one. Vietnam is one. Nigeria, in a different sense. South Africa. And that's all within my lifetime. If you go back further, every country in Europe during World War II was traumatized to one degree or another. ANINDO ATIK When I was researching at the Library of Congress, I went through newspapers from different countries from 1971 onward. And I saw how particularly this war is neglected. It makes me think — I still wonder why. PETER KANN If you say 1971, at least to Americans, there was a war going on in Vietnam. There was a war going on in Cambodia. There were, as usual, crises in the Middle East. The drama of the situation in East Pakistan, which didn't really get very much coverage, probably didn't affect very many Americans at all. And the war, when it finally took place, was very brief. So yes, that would have created headlines in December 1971. But then Bangladesh went back to becoming a sort of anonymous country. I've never been back now. There are a lot of places I haven't been back to in Asia. But part of the reason I didn't go back to Bangladesh later in the 1970s, when I was still living in Asia, was because I kind of thought, "Gee, if I go back, I'm going to write sort of sad stories about progress that isn't taking place or isn't taking place fast enough, or political fights." I was very sympathetic to the Bengalis, and I really wanted them to succeed. So, I didn't want to go back and write about lack of success. Now, I think these days probably there's been enough success that one could be more hopeful. But for quite a while there wasn't. ✦ CLOSING ✦ CLOSING Peter Kann's Reflections on His Reporting ANINDO ATIK Your reporting was very comprehensive. Very different from others. PETER KANN I had the advantage that others probably did not have — I could choose what stories I wanted to write. I didn't get assigned stories by an editor. And I wrote most of the stories 2,000 words or longer. Sometimes more. And I could write them infrequently, so I could spend two or three weeks reporting a story and then sit down and write. Most newspapers want their reporters to write, if not every day, every other day. They want stories that are 800 words, not 2,000 words. My newspaper was very good to work for. And that was true in Bangladesh but elsewhere too. In Bangladesh, they were so nice they wanted me to leave. When the evacuation flights took place in December, my editors told me to get on the evacuation flight and leave. And obviously I didn't want to leave. I wanted to see the end of the story. So, I stayed. And I think I sent them a cable saying, "Regarding leaving Dhaka, your cable unreceived" — or something like that. So that became a funny story later. But yeah, I should give credit to my paper. They were great to work for. ANINDO ATIK To me, you and people like you — I think I'm going to admire you guys all my life. PETER KANN Oh, well, you're very kind. We tried to bring it to the attention of America, at least. “Regarding leaving Dhaka, your cable unreceived.” — PETER KANN’S CABLE TO HIS WALL STREET JOURNAL EDITORS, WHEN ASKED TO EVACUATE DHAKA DURING THE DECEMBER 1971 WAR — SO HE COULD WITNESS LIBERATION DAY.
